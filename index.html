One tank of water does five jobs

On the Moon, every kilogram is shipped at great cost, so our habitat makes the same water work over and over: it shields the crew, raises fish, grows plants, cools equipment and is recycled back. This portal holds the engineering behind each step, with calculators you can try.

Water pumping systems

Water is the habitat's most reused resource. The design goal is to lose as little as possible and to keep working when a pump or pipe fails.

Storage and distribution

Water is stored in the shielding walls around the sleep pods and storm shelter, so the same mass protects the crew. Two separate loops (drinking and technical) stop contamination from spreading.

If it fails: a valve can isolate any wall section, so one leak never drains the whole store.

Filtration

Particle filters, then multifiltration beds and a catalytic reactor, as on the ISS. Drinking water is checked for conductivity and microbes before it is used.

If it fails: bad water is held in a quarantine tank, never sent to the taps. Filters are swapped on a set schedule.

Greywater recycling

The ISS urine processor was designed to recover 85% of the water in urine and now reaches 87%; with the brine processor, the whole system reaches about 98%.

If it fails: the crew switch to stored water and wipes for washing while it is repaired.

Emergency backup pumps

Every critical loop has a second pump (N+1). The fish tanks have battery-backed aeration, because fish die in hours without oxygen, much faster than plants suffer.

If it fails: the backup takes over automatically and the crew is alerted.

Leak detection

Flow meters on every branch are compared in a mass balance: water pumped in should equal water measured out. A gap means a leak. Humidity sensors back this up.

If it fails: slow leaks hide inside walls, so tank levels are checked against the balance every day.

What it costs

Every kilogram of water we recycle is a kilogram that doesn't have to be launched. A 98% loop loses only 2 kg in every 100 kg used.

Fluid dynamics laboratory

Gravity on the Moon is 1.62 m/s², one sixth of Earth's. Water still flows through pipes much as it does at home, but anything that depends on gravity (pressure from depth, bubbles rising, warm air rising) changes. Try it:

Open the fluid lab

Hydrostatic pressure = density × g × height. Bubble rise for small bubbles scales with g (Stokes' law). Natural convection heat transfer scales roughly with g to the power 1/3.

Low-gravity water behaviour

Water still pools at the bottom of tanks on the Moon, unlike in orbit. But it sloshes more slowly and surface tension matters more, so small channels and wicks behave differently than on Earth.

Pressure management

A tall tank pushes down with only one sixth of the pressure it would on Earth, so pumps barely have to lift water. Most pump power goes into friction in the pipes, and friction doesn't depend on gravity.

Heat transfer

Warm air rises more weakly, so electronics and grow lights cool less on their own. Fans and pumped coolant must do the work. That costs power and adds failure points.

CFD simulations

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) lets us test airflow around sleeping crew (CO₂ can pool near a face without fans), tank sloshing and coolant flow before building anything. Results still need checking against a real test.

Pipe network optimisation

Shorter, wider pipes cut friction losses. Loops (rather than dead-end branches) stop stagnant water, where microbes grow. Every branch gets an isolation valve.

Open questions

How do gas bubbles separate from water in fish tanks and filters at 1/6 g? How does waste settle? These have never been tested on the Moon and are good experiments for a future mission.

Radiation protection centre

The Moon has no magnetic field or atmosphere. China's Chang'e 4 lander measured an average dose equivalent of 1,369 µSv per day on the surface, about 2.6 times the dose inside the ISS.

Shielding cuts galactic cosmic rays only partly, because high-energy particles make secondary radiation in the shield. Solar storms are stopped far better. Set the reduction to match your design and see how it changes the total.

Galactic cosmic rays

A steady rain of very high-energy particles from outside the solar system. They cannot be fully blocked; shielding and limiting time on the surface are the tools.

Solar particle events

Bursts of protons from the Sun that can arrive within hours. They are much easier to block than cosmic rays, which is why the habitat has a storm shelter.

Water shielding

Hydrogen-rich materials like water are among the best shields per kilogram. Our design target is a 20 cm water wall (200 kg per m²) around the shelter, using water the habitat needs anyway.

Regolith shielding

Lunar soil is free on site. Piling it over the modules adds a lot of mass without launching any, but it needs robots or machines to move.

Storm shelter analysis

The medical module doubles as the shelter. All four crew must fit with 72 hours of supplies, and EVAs stay close enough to get back inside after a solar warning.

If it fails: a drained water wall is never allowed below shielding level, even during a water shortage.

Career limit

NASA limits an astronaut's career dose to 600 mSv. At the surface rate above, an unshielded astronaut would reach it in about 440 days, so shielding decides how long missions can be.

Food production and aquaponics centre

Research question: for a long stay on the Moon, does adding fish to a plant farm (aquaponics) beat growing plants alone (hydroponics)?

Option A: hydroponics only

Plants grow in a nutrient solution under LEDs. NASA's Biomass Production Chamber grew wheat, soybean, lettuce, potato and tomato this way.

Pros: simpler, fewer biological risks, less maintenance, lower contamination risk. Cons: protein must come from crops like soybean or from Earth, and the diet is less varied.

Option B: aquaponics (fish + plants)

Fish waste is turned into nitrate by bacteria in a biofilter; plants take up the nitrate and clean the water, which returns to the fish.

Pros: fresh protein and vegetables, water recycled continuously, less waste. Cons: more water, power and parts; fish feed must be imported or grown; fish die quickly if aeration fails.

Water quality to monitor

pH (a compromise near 7 suits fish, bacteria and plants), dissolved oxygen, ammonia, nitrate and temperature. Each has a sensor and an alarm.

Lunar gravity effects to study

How fish swim and orient, how water circulates, how oxygen spreads and how waste particles settle at 1/6 g. Fish flown in orbit have swum in loops; nobody has kept fish at lunar gravity.
